Rei nforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful approach for healthcare decision support due to its ability to model sequential treatment decisions and optimize long-term clinical outcomes. However, many existing healthcare RL implementations focus predominantly on maximizing clinical rewards while implicitly assuming uniform treatment affordability and patient adherence. In real-world healthcare systems, socioeconomic constraints particularly treatment cost relative to patient economic capacity play a critical role in adherence, continuity of care, and eventual health outcomes. Ignoring these factors can result in reinforcement learning policies that disproportionately favour economically advantaged patients, thereby reinforcing socioeconomic disparities. This paper presents a technical reinforcement learning implementation that explicitly incorporates socioeconomic fairness into the policy learning process. We model healthcare decision-making as an episodic Markov Decision Process in which patient state transitions are influenced by both clinical response and cost-induced adherence dynamics. The proposed system implements a complete fairness-constrained RL pipeline using Q-learning with epsilon-greedy exploration, experience replay, and fairness-aware reward shaping. This work demonstrates the practical feasibility of embedding ethical and socioeconomic considerations directly into reinforcement learning–based healthcare decision support systems, contributing toward the development of more equitable and socially responsible healthcare AI. `n`n `n`n `n`n Keywords: Reinforcement Learning, Ethical AI, Socioeconomic Fairness, Healthcare Decision Support, Algorithmic Bias, Fairness-Constrained Optimization `n`n`n `n`n`n `n`n`n `n`n`n `n`n`n Introduction `n`n`n `n`n`n Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become an increasingly important paradigm for healthcare decision support, owing to its ability to model sequential decision-making processes under uncertainty. Unlike traditional supervised learning approaches that operate on static input–output mappings, reinforcement learning explicitly captures the long-term consequences of treatment decisions by learning policies that optimize cumulative outcomes over time. This makes RL particularly well suited for healthcare applications involving chronic disease management, treatment sequencing, personalized therapy selection, and adaptive clinical decision support, where present actions influence future patient states and outcomes. `n`n`n `n`n`n Despite this suitability, many existing healthcare reinforcement learning implementations are primarily designed to optimize clinical performance metrics such as symptom improvement, survival probability, or biomarker stabilization. These systems are commonly trained on retrospective clinical datasets and employ reward functions that focus exclusively on physiological outcomes. While clinically relevant, such formulations implicitly assume that all recommended treatments are equally affordable and that patients are uniformly able to adhere to prescribed care pathways. In real-world healthcare settings, these assumptions are rarely valid. `n`n`n `n`n`n Socioeconomic factors including treatment cost, insurance coverage, and out-of-pocket expenditure play a decisive role in shaping patient adherence and continuity of care. Treatment strategies that are clinically optimal but financially burdensome often lead to reduced adherence, delayed intervention, or complete discontinuation, particularly among economically vulnerable populations. Reinforcement learning agents that ignore these constraints risk producing recommendations that perform well in simulation or retrospective evaluation but fail when applied to heterogeneous patient populations. `n`n`n `n`n`n This challenge is further exacerbated by the data-driven nature of reinforcement learning. Observational healthcare datasets tend to disproportionately represent patients with consistent access to care and higher economic capacity, resulting in cleaner and more complete treatment histories. When trained on such data, reinforcement learning agents may implicitly learn policies that favor these populations, leading to feedback loops that amplify socioeconomic disparities over time. As a result, reinforcement learning systems risk reinforcing existing inequities rather than mitigating them. `n`n`n `n`n`n This work argues that optimizing clinical outcomes alone is insufficient for responsible healthcare reinforcement learning. Instead, socioeconomic feasibility must be treated as a first-class component of the learning objective. To address this need, we present a technical reinforcement learning implementation that embeds affordability-aware fairness constraints directly into policy learning. The proposed system models healthcare decision-making as an episodic Markov Decision Process in which patient state transitions reflect both clinical response and cost-induced adherence effects. A Relative Financial Burden metric is introduced to quantify cumulative treatment cost relative to patient economic capacity, while population-level inequality is measured using the Gini coefficient and incorporated directly into the reward function. `n`n`n `n`n`n The primary contribution of this work lies in demonstrating, through implementation, how healthcare reinforcement learning can be reframed as a joint clinical–economic optimization problem. By integrating socioeconomic fairness into the reward structure during training rather than applying post hoc bias corrections, this study provides a practical and technically grounded pathway toward ethically responsible and socially sustainable reinforcement learning–based healthcare decision support systems. `n`n`n `n`n`n Application and Significance `n`n`n `n`n`n Reinforcement learning–based decision support systems have been increasingly adopted in healthcare due to their ability to model sequential treatment decisions and optimize long-term outcomes. However, the practical effectiveness of such systems in real-world clinical environments is closely tied to socioeconomic factors, including treatment affordability, patient access to care, and cost-driven adherence behavior. Healthcare reinforcement learning systems that ignore these factors risk producing policies that perform well under idealized assumptions but fail to generalize across economically diverse patient populations. This section discusses application domains where socioeconomic-aware reinforcement learning is particularly relevant and outlines the broader significance of integrating fairness constraints directly into healthcare RL implementations. `n`n`n `n`n`n Applications of Socioeconomically Aware Reinforcement Learning `n`n`n One of the most impactful applications of reinforcement learning in healthcare is personalized treatment planning for chronic diseases , where patients require continuous and adaptive care over extended time horizons. Conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and chronic respiratory disorders involve repeated treatment decisions that influence both immediate clinical response and long-term health trajectories. While reinforcement learning is well suited to optimizing such treatment sequences, implementations that ignore affordability may recommend high-cost interventions that patients are unable to sustain. The proposed fairness-constrained RL system addresses this limitation by modeling treatment cost and economic capacity within the environment, enabling the learned policy to balance clinical benefit with financial sustainability. `n`n`n In clinical decision support systems, reinforcement learning has been applied to assist clinicians in selecting treatment options, adjusting dosages, and scheduling follow-up interventions. By explicitly incorporating cost-induced adherence dynamics and fairness-aware reward shaping, the proposed implementation supports decision-making that accounts for patient-specific economic constraints. This approach facilitates more realistic and actionable recommendations, reducing the likelihood of treatment discontinuation due to financial burden. `n`n`n At the healthcare system level, reinforcement learning has been explored for optimizing resource allocation, care prioritization, and treatment planning under budget constraints. Fairness-aware reinforcement learning enables system-level policies to optimize outcomes without disproportionately favoring economically advantaged patient groups. By penalizing inequality in financial burden distribution, the proposed system provides a mechanism for promoting equitable access to healthcare resources across diverse socioeconomic populations. `n`n`n Finally, public health and preventive care applications can benefit from affordability-aware reinforcement learning. Policies that consider economic accessibility can inform the design of intervention strategies, screening schedules, and long-term prevention programs, particularly in underserved communities where cost barriers are a primary determinant of care utilization. The implemented framework demonstrates how reinforcement learning can be extended beyond clinical efficacy to support equitable and sustainable healthcare decision-making. `n`n`n `n`n`n Significance of the Proposed Approach `n`n The significance of this work lies in demonstrating, through a concrete technical implementation, how socioeconomic fairness can be embedded directly into reinforcement learning–based healthcare decision support systems. By integrating affordability metrics and inequality-based penalties into the reward function during training, the proposed approach aligns algorithmic optimization with real-world feasibility and ethical considerations. This implementation-driven perspective advances healthcare reinforcement learning beyond performance-centric objectives and contributes toward the development of socially responsible, practically deployable healthcare AI systems. `n`n From an ethical perspective, embedding fairness directly into the learning objective operationalizes principles of equity and distributive justice within reinforcement learning. This prevents the reinforcement of structural biases that may arise from historical data and feedback-driven policy learning. Practically, the approach enhances patient adherence, clinician trust, and real-world deployability of healthcare AI systems. `n`n Overall, integrating socioeconomic fairness into reinforcement learning transforms healthcare decision support from a purely performance-driven optimization task into a human-centered, ethically grounded system capable of delivering equitable and sustainable clinical outcomes. `n`n`n `n`n`n Background Study `n`n `n`n This section reviews prior research relevant to reinforcement learning in healthcare, with particular emphasis on clinical decision support, safety-aware learning, and fairness in machine learning. The discussion highlights how existing approaches have advanced sequential decision-making in medical settings while also exposing critical limitations related to socioeconomic bias and affordability, which motivate the need for the proposed framework. A detailed study is given in Table 3.1. `n`n `n`n`n 3.1 Safe Reinforcement Learning via Episodic Control `n`n`n Li, Zhu, and Grossklags proposed an episodic control–based framework to address one of the central challenges of safe reinforcement learning: poor sample efficiency under safety constraints. Traditional RL methods require extensive interaction with the environment, which is infeasible in healthcare due to ethical and safety considerations. The authors mitigate this limitation by introducing state abstraction mechanisms that discretize continuous state spaces into representative clusters, enabling efficient reuse of past experiences. `n`n`n A key contribution of this work is the joint optimization of reward maximization and constraint satisfaction using a Pareto-front formulation. By explicitly modeling both objectives, the method avoids collapsing safety into a secondary penalty term. Empirical evaluations demonstrate improved learning efficiency and reduced constraint violations compared to baseline approaches. However, the reliance on state abstraction introduces sensitivity to clustering quality, which may limit scalability in high-dimensional clinical settings where patient heterogeneity is pronounced. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.2 Safe Policies for Reinforcement Learning via Primal-Dual Methods `n`n`n Paternain et al. addressed safety-constrained reinforcement learning in scenarios where system dynamics are unknown, a common condition in healthcare. The authors formulated safety constraints probabilistically and introduced a primal–dual optimization framework that transforms hard constraints into a tractable Lagrangian objective. This relaxation enables gradient-based optimization without requiring explicit transition models. `n`n`n The strength of this approach lies in its theoretical grounding, offering formal guarantees on constraint satisfaction under mild assumptions. Unlike heuristic penalty-based methods, the primal–dual formulation provides principled control over safety–performance trade-offs. Nevertheless, the approach assumes sufficiently expressive policy parameterizations and may face convergence challenges in highly nonconvex healthcare environments, particularly when reward and safety objectives are strongly conflicting. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.3 Reinforcement Learning for Intelligent Healthcare Systems: A Review of Challenges, Applications, and Open Research Issues `n`n`n Abdellatif et al. presented a comprehensive survey of reinforcement learning applications across intelligent healthcare architectures, spanning edge intelligence, core network optimization, and clinical decision support. The work highlights the suitability of RL for managing uncertainty, latency, and dynamic system behavior in healthcare infrastructures. `n`n`n While the survey effectively maps algorithmic choices to healthcare layers, it largely emphasizes performance metrics and system efficiency, with comparatively limited focus on safety guarantees and fairness considerations. As a result, it underscores the need for deeper integration of ethical and clinical constraints into healthcare RL frameworks rather than treating them as secondary design concerns. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.4 Fair Machine Learning in Healthcare: A Survey `n`n`n Feng et al. provided a foundational analysis of fairness in healthcare machine learning grounded in principles of distributive justice. The study categorizes fairness challenges into equal allocation and equal performance, offering a structured lens for evaluating algorithmic equity. Importantly, the authors trace sources of bias across the entire ML lifecycle, from data collection to deployment. `n`n`n Although the survey offers extensive coverage of fairness metrics and mitigation strategies, its primary focus is on supervised learning paradigms. Extending these fairness concepts to reinforcement learning where decisions affect future states and data distributions introduces additional complexity that remains insufficiently explored. This gap is particularly critical for healthcare applications involving long-term treatment policies. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.5 A Reinforcement Learning Model for Optimal Treatment Strategies in Intensive Care `n`n`n Drudi et al. proposed reinforcement learning–based frameworks for optimizing treatment strategies in intensive care units, with particular emphasis on cardiorespiratory dynamics and physiological feature importance. Their work demonstrates that RL models can learn complex, temporally dependent treatment policies from ICU data, identifying clinically meaningful state variables that influence therapeutic decisions. `n`n`n A notable contribution of this line of research is the incorporation of feature importance analysis, which partially addresses clinician concerns regarding interpretability. However, these studies primarily rely on retrospective observational datasets and counterfactual evaluations. As a result, the causal validity and generalizability of the learned policies across diverse ICU populations remain open questions, underscoring the need for prospective clinical validation. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.6 Warfarin Dose Management Using Offline Deep Reinforcement Learning `n`n`n Ji et al. investigated the application of offline deep reinforcement learning for personalized warfarin dose management using historical electronic health records. By restricting learning to pre-collected data, their approach eliminates the need for online exploration, which is infeasible in high-risk anticoagulation therapy. `n`n`n The results indicate that offline RL can achieve dosing strategies comparable to, or in some cases exceeding, expert clinician performance while maintaining patients within therapeutic ranges. Despite these promising outcomes, the approach remains sensitive to dataset bias and distributional shift. Performance degradation may occur when deployment populations differ substantially from training cohorts, highlighting the importance of robust off-policy evaluation and uncertainty quantification. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.7 Adaptive Decision Transformer for Dynamic Treatment Regimes in Sepsis `n`n`n Jeon et al. introduced Adaptive Decision Transformer (ADT) architectures tailored for dynamic treatment regimes in sepsis management. By leveraging transformer-based attention mechanisms, ADTs model clinical decision-making as a sequence prediction problem, enabling the identification of prior observations that most strongly influence current treatment actions. `n`n`n This architecture improves interpretability relative to conventional deep RL methods and aligns well with clinician reasoning processes. However, transformer-based approaches typically lack explicit safety guarantees and may struggle with long-horizon credit assignment. Integrating attention-based models with formal safety constraints and fairness-aware objectives remains an important open research direction. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.8 Exploring Bias and Prediction Metrics to Characterize Fairness of Machine Learning for Equity-Centered Public Health Decision-Making `n`n`n Raza et al. examined bias and fairness in public health machine learning systems, emphasizing the limitations of aggregate performance metrics in capturing inequities across demographic subgroups. Their work highlights how conventional evaluation practices can mask disparities in sensitivity, specificity, and predictive utility among vulnerable populations. `n`n`n While the study provides valuable insights into equity-centered evaluation, its focus is primarily on static predictive models. Extending these fairness considerations to reinforcement learning systems introduces additional challenges, as policy decisions influence future health outcomes and data distributions. This limitation underscores the need for fairness metrics specifically designed for sequential and adaptive decision-making. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.9 Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning Based Healthcare Expansion Planning Considering Pandemic Events `n`n`n Shuvo et al. applied multi-objective reinforcement learning to healthcare infrastructure expansion and capacity planning during pandemic scenarios. Their framework balances competing objectives such as infection control, economic cost, infrastructure development, and equitable access to care. `n`n`n Although effective at the system-planning level, these approaches operate at a high level of abstraction and do not directly address patient-level safety or clinical interpretability. Bridging the gap between strategic planning models and individualized clinical decision support remains a non-trivial limitation for healthcare RL research. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.10 AoI-Aware Resource Management for Smart Health Via Deep Reinforcement Learning `n`n`n Wu et al. proposed deep reinforcement learning approaches for Age-of-Information (AoI)–aware resource management in smart healthcare systems. Their work optimizes monitoring and communication policies to balance information freshness against computational and communication costs, ensuring timely detection of patient deterioration. `n`n`n `n`n`n `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.11 Reinforcement Learning-Based Integrated Risk-Aware Dynamic Treatment Strategy for Consumer-Centric Next-Gen Healthcare `n`n`n In parallel, Nimma et al. developed risk-aware reinforcement learning frameworks that explicitly incorporate patient risk into treatment optimization objectives. While both approaches align algorithmic behavior with clinical priorities, risk modeling is often simplified, and interactions between risk, fairness, and long-term outcomes are insufficiently explored. `n`n`n `n`n`n 3.12 Pruning the Way to Reliable Policies: A Multi-Objective Deep Q-Learning Approach to Critical Care `n`n`n Shirali et al. introduced a multi-objective deep Q-learning framework with policy pruning to improve reliability and interpretability in critical care decision-making. By identifying and removing unstable or spurious learned behaviors, the method enhances policy robustness and clinical trust. `n`n`n Despite these advances, scalability to high-dimensional state spaces and robustness under clinical variability remain unresolved. Additionally, balancing multiple competing objectives under strict safety constraints continues to pose significant algorithmic challenges. `n`n`n Table 3.1 presents a comprehensive summary of all the related works which has already been done on Reinforcement Learning in Healthcare and Financial Decision Making. `n`n`n Table 3.1.
